Buying off-plan (VEFA) in Morocco: how it works

How an off-plan (VEFA) purchase works in Morocco: the steps, the preliminary contract, guarantees in general terms and the documents to ask the developer for.

What is a VEFA?

A VEFA (vente en l'état futur d'achèvement) is an off-plan sale: you buy a property that is not finished yet, commit to it and pay in stages while it is built, then it is handed over to you. In Morocco, this type of sale is governed by law 107-12.

In practice, you buy on the strength of plans, a written specification and a schedule. Until the property is handed over, it is the paperwork that protects your purchase: the contract, the guarantees and the documents showing that the developer is entitled to build and to sell.

The appeal of buying off-plan is that you choose early and spread the payments over time; the risk is paying for something that does not exist yet. The whole point is therefore to check before you pay, not afterwards.

The steps of an off-plan purchase

Typically: choosing the unit, a reservation, the preliminary contract, staged payments during construction, handover, then the final deed at the notary's office. The exact order is to be confirmed with your notary for each development.

  • Choosing the unit: plans, floor area, floor level, orientation and the finishes listed in the specification.
  • Reservation: a deposit is sometimes requested. Its terms (refundable or not, how long it stays valid) should be in writing before you pay anything.
  • Preliminary contract: it sets out the property, the price, the payment schedule and the expected delivery date.
  • Staged payments: instalments are called in line with the progress of the works set out in the contract.
  • Handover: an inspection visit and a list of defects for the developer to put right.
  • Final deed and the formalities that transfer ownership, handled by the notary.

Document names, timings and sequencing can vary from one development to another. Ask the developer for the full timeline in writing, then have your notary confirm it.

The preliminary contract and the guarantees

The preliminary contract describes what you are buying, at what price, on what timetable and with what protections. You should expect a guarantee that either refunds the sums you have paid or ensures the works are completed; its exact form is to be confirmed with your notary.

It is the contract, not the sales brochure, that binds the seller. Read it in full before paying anything beyond the reservation deposit. At a minimum it should allow you to identify:

  • the unit you are buying (number, floor, floor areas), with the plans and the specification attached;
  • the price, what it includes (parking, storage room, terrace, fittings) and the payment schedule;
  • the expected delivery date and the consequences of a delay;
  • the cases in which either you or the developer can cancel or withdraw;
  • the guarantee: what kind it is, who issues it and what it covers.

Guarantees, in general terms

A refund guarantee is meant to return the sums you have paid if the sale does not go through; a completion guarantee is meant to see the works finished even if the developer fails. Ask for the document itself rather than a mere mention in a brochure, and have your notary check that it matches your development and your contract.

The documents to ask the developer for

Before paying, ask for three essential documents: the land title, the building permit and the guarantee document, then compare them with the contract you are offered.

  • Land title, or a recent certificate of ownership: it shows who owns the land and whether any charges are registered against it (mortgage, dispute). The owner should be the company that is selling; if not, ask for the document that explains why.
  • Building permit: it confirms the project is authorised. Check that the number of units and the floor areas match what is being sold.
  • Guarantee document: the text itself, who issues it, what it covers and from when.
  • Draft preliminary contract, plans and written specification, to read before signing anything.
  • Payment schedule and the planned works timetable.
  • Identity of the selling company (registered name, trade register number) and the name of the notary who will act.

The name of the selling company, the name on the land title and the name in the contract must match, as must the floor areas across the plans, the permit and the contract. Any difference should be explained in writing.

A serious developer provides these documents without difficulty. Reluctance or vague answers are information in themselves, and it is better to notice them before you pay.

How KEYPLAN's TRUST score checks these points

The TRUST score rates a development's file out of 100, based on eleven items grouped into five criteria. Three items are vital: the land title, the building or subdivision permit and the completion or refund guarantee. If any of them is not verified, the development has no score and shows “Incomplete file”.

  • Land title (25 points): title registered in the name of the developer or its project company, no registered dispute or charge.
  • Track record (15 points): delivered developments verified, delivery deadlines met.
  • Urban planning (20 points): building or subdivision permit, development as marketed matching the permit.
  • Off-plan safeguards (25 points): preliminary contract compliant with law 107-12, completion guarantee or guarantee of refund of the sums paid.
  • Transparency (15 points): published plans, specification and prices, documents provided on request.

A verified item earns its full points, a partly verified item half of them, an item that is not verified or not disclosed none. The total gives the score, shown with its tier: from 80 to 100 “Excellent file”, from 65 to 79 “Good file”, from 0 to 64 “File to be completed”.

On each development's page, KEYPLAN shows the state of each item (verified, partly verified or not verified), its points and the date it was last checked. The score helps you compare developments and prepare your questions; it replaces neither a careful reading of the contract nor your notary's advice. The scoring rules, the sources consulted and the process (pre-score sent to the developer, right of reply, quarterly review) are set out on the TRUST methodology page.

Questions to put to the notary

Have a notary review the contract and the documents before any significant payment. These questions cover the essentials.

  • Is the land title free of charges and registrations?
  • Does the preliminary contract comply with the legal framework for off-plan sales?
  • Which guarantee covers my payments, and what happens if the works stop?
  • Are my payments tied to the actual progress of the works, and into which account should I make them?
  • Which costs and formalities remain my responsibility, and when?
  • What does the contract provide for in case of a delay or a defect at handover?

Prepare these questions before the meeting and keep the answers in writing: they will serve as your reference throughout the project.

Frequently asked questions

What does VEFA stand for?

VEFA stands for vente en l'état futur d'achèvement, the French term for the sale of a property that is under construction or not yet built, with delivery at a later date. In English this is usually called an off-plan purchase.

Is the reservation deposit refundable?

It depends on the terms written in the reservation document: how long it lasts, the grounds for a refund and any amount withheld. Ask for them in writing before paying, and have a notary look at them if they are unclear.

What happens if delivery is late?

The preliminary contract should state the expected delivery date and the consequences of a delay. If it does not, clarify it before signing. The remedies available to you are to be confirmed with your notary.

Does a high TRUST score mean I do not need a notary?

No. The TRUST score helps you compare developments on verifiable documents, but it replaces neither a careful reading of the contract nor a notary's advice.
